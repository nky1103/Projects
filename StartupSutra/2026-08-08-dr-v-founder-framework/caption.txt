Rheumatoid arthritis twisted his fingers so badly he couldn't hold a pen. He went on to build the world's largest eye hospital, and give sight back to millions.

This is the story of Dr. Govindappa Venkataswamy, known simply as Dr. V.

At 30, arthritis left him bedridden for over a year. His hands were crippled. Most people would have left medicine. Instead, he taught himself to hold a scalpel again and trained as a cataract surgeon.

Then, at 58, the age most people retire, he started over. With an 11-bed clinic in Madurai, no money, no business plan and no safety net, he set out to do something that sounded impossible: end needless blindness in India.

Here is the framework he built, and why it still matters for every founder.

One: engineer compassion like an operation. He asked a strange question. If McDonald's can sell billions of burgers cheaply and consistently, why can't we do the same for sight-restoring surgery? So he turned cataract surgery into a high-volume, high-quality, low-cost assembly line. One patient on the table, the next already prepped. He personally performed over 100 surgeries a day.

Two: let those who can pay, fund those who can't. No patient is ever turned away. Those who can afford it pay a fair price. Roughly 60% receive care free or at a steep discount. And here is the twist that stuns people: the hospital still runs a surplus. It never had to beg for donations. It was self-reliant by design.

The result: over 100,000 surgeries by his own crippled hands, millions of people given their sight back, and Aravind Eye Care grew into the largest eye-care network in the world.

The lesson: a mission scales only when you engineer it like a business. Dr. V didn't just care. He made caring efficient enough to reach millions. Purpose and profit were never enemies. They funded each other.
