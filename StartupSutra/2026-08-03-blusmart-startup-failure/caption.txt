Riders loved BluSmart. Then it vanished overnight. And it didn't die because electric cars don't make money. It died for a reason most people miss.

BluSmart was India's all-electric ride-hailing service, an Uber rival across Delhi NCR, Mumbai and Bengaluru. Clean cars, no surge pricing, fixed fares, drivers on salary instead of being squeezed. Riders were genuinely loyal, and around 10,000 drivers depended on it. Demand was never the problem.

Here's the fatal flaw. BluSmart didn't own its cars. Its entire fleet was leased from Gensol, a separate, publicly listed company run by the same co-founder. One critical dependency, sitting in related-party hands.

Then it unravelled. Gensol had taken around ₹978 crore in loans (from IREDA and PFC) to buy EVs for BluSmart. A SEBI investigation alleged that a large chunk, over ₹200 crore, was diverted to entities linked to the promoters, reportedly even a luxury apartment. SEBI barred the Jaggi brothers from the markets.

The domino fell fast. With Gensol under scrutiny and its financing frozen, BluSmart lost access to its own fleet. It simply couldn't operate. Services stopped, and thousands of drivers were stranded overnight.

Read that again: the product was fine. The structure and governance around it killed it.

The lesson for founders is uncomfortable but important. A great product cannot survive bad governance. Never let a related party control the one thing your business depends on. Governance isn't paperwork you do for investors, it's the thing that keeps you alive.
